\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\PARstart{B}{one} X-ray imaging is widely used in screening, diagnosis support, and follow-up because it is fast, inexpensive, and broadly available. Lesion segmentation in radiographs, however, remains difficult when abnormalities are very small, faint, or partially obscured by surrounding anatomy. In such settings, failure often begins at localization: before any boundary can be refined, the model must first identify a tiny suspicious region within the full image.

This is why small-lesion segmentation is rarely just a contouring problem. When the lesion occupies only a small fraction of all pixels, searching the whole radiograph is inefficient and error-prone. A natural response is to reduce the search space first, then segment inside the narrowed region. Fully automatic methods try to learn this process internally. U-Net and Attention U-Net \cite{b3,b4} remain important examples of that route, but they still require the network to localize and segment the lesion from scratch.

An alternative is to provide region-of-interest guidance externally. In clinical use, a radiologist can often draw a rough box around a suspicious area more easily than a fully automatic model can detect a very small lesion reliably. In that sense, a box prompt is more than a convenience for interaction; it is a practical way to inject spatial reasoning into the pipeline before detailed segmentation begins. Prompt-based and interactive methods build on this idea, from clicks and scribbles \cite{b5,b6} to recent promptable foundation models such as SAM and SAM-Med2D \cite{b1,b2}.

For the present task, however, one issue remains unresolved. Once a prompt has already localized the approximate region, should it serve only as a positional constraint for mask generation, or should it continue to shape feature extraction so that weak lesion evidence is not washed out by downsampling? This distinction is especially important for very small lesions, where a minor spatial drift can matter as much as the boundary itself.

We address this question with PGA-UNet, a lightweight prompt-guided CNN that treats the box prompt as a structured spatial prior rather than an auxiliary input channel. The prompt is encoded as a plateau-style Gaussian heatmap, injected into encoder features through a Prompt Spatial Gate (PSG), and reused in the decoder through a Conditional Attention Decoder (CAD). The intent is to keep prompt-guided lesion evidence active throughout the network, not merely to segment within a predefined box.

This view also determines how the experiments are interpreted. Attention U-Net receives no clinician-provided spatial cue, so its role is to show how difficult the problem becomes without prompt guidance. The prompt-matched comparison is with SAM-Med2D, which receives the same box prompt. The central question is therefore not whether prompts help in general, but whether a lightweight architecture can use a coarse clinician prompt more effectively by preserving weak lesion evidence instead of treating the prompt as a simple spatial restriction.

The main contributions of this article are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item We present a compact prompt-guided architecture for interactive bone X-ray lesion segmentation.
\item We integrate plateau-style Gaussian prompting, encoder-side prompt gating, and decoder-side prompt-conditioned attention within a lightweight network.
\item We formulate an offline training and online interactive inference framework for prompt-guided radiographic lesion segmentation, with explicit mathematical definitions for prompt encoding, encoder gating, and decoder conditioning.
\item We evaluate the method on BTXRD and FracAtlas under covering and off-center prompt settings, including comparisons with Attention U-Net and SAM-Med2D, a small-lesion analysis with Dice, a center-based localization score (CBL), and HD95, Monte Carlo cross-validation, and efficiency measurements.
\item We report ablation, efficiency, and small-lesion results that clarify where the proposed prompt-conditioning design is most useful.
\end{itemize}
